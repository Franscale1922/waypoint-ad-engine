# Waypoint Story Bible — v1 (2026-09-27)

_The creative ceiling for every Waypoint ad. Also the fixed system prompt for the script model, so it
contains fiction rules only: no client, candidate or prospect data ever goes in here or near it.
Sources: `CLAUDE.md` (binding), `research/REFERENCE-DIGEST-batch4-positioning.md`, batch 2 Addenda
A + C, digest v1 §3. Visual style is NOT set here; a bake-off decides it (BRIEF T4)._

## 00. Emotion first (Kelsey, 2026-09-29, overrides anything below that conflicts)
Every ad opens with an emotional story that binds the viewer before any Waypoint message or service
appears. Wit and sarcasm are texture inside that story, never a substitute for it. First audit
question for every concept: does the first 20 seconds make a 50-year-old feel something before they
know it's an ad? A clever concept that fails this fails.

## 0. What a Waypoint ad is
A 30–60 second piece of animated fiction about one person, 40–60, in the last stretch of a corporate
life, that ends on a decision and a way to find out more. It is not about brands, money or Waypoint.
It is about the buyer's inner life, which nobody else in franchising is filming. Facebook feed,
9:16 or 1:1, sound on or off. The person watching should feel seen first and sold to never.

## 1. Stances (decided by Kelsey; every ad carries at least one, said or shown)
1. Most people shouldn't buy a franchise, and a good advisor tells them so.
2. Your employer isn't the villain. Your waiting is.
3. Picking a brand first is backwards. Figure out what you want your Tuesday to look like.
4. Meet brands at an expo. Never choose one there.
5. "Passive income" is mostly a lie; plan to work.
A stance is an opinion about ideas. It is never a promise about outcomes.

## 2. The average, banned
**Franchise content:** "open a [brand] for just $X" · brand-vs-brand · "X franchises that fail" ·
"what nobody tells you / the dark side" · top/best/most-profitable lists · success-rate percentages ·
"no money down" · "$X with 10 hours a week", semi-absentee, passive income · fast-food brand bait ·
talking head in office or car, storefront b-roll, money in the title · cheerleader, hype investor,
grim skeptic · CTAs: free consultation, "check territory availability", free guide, "comment
FRANCHISE" · words: financial freedom, be your own boss, proven system, blueprint, playbook,
recession-proof, empire, journey, right fit.
**Midlife-exit content:** "laid off at [age] after [N] years" · wound → "blessing in disguise" /
"new chapter" · faith-inflected earnestness · tearful confession to camera · faceless ocean b-roll
with sad piano · "companies that WANT you" listicles · smug "should've saved" · AI-voiced revenge
fiction with cartoon justice.
**Compliance (never, even from a fictional character):** any dollar figure, payback period,
hours-per-week or income outcome · revenue implied as income · top-performer stories · scarcity ·
real people's likeness or voice · a fictional story presented as real.
**Adjectives banned in scripts and prompts:** cinematic, emotional, warm, beautiful, stunning,
powerful, journey, dream. Replace each with a noun, an action, an object or a named light source.
**Rule 1, every piece:** before writing, name in two lines what 100 other ads would do. Those
choices are now banned for this piece. Log them in the ad's record.

## 3. Territories (the unclaimed ground, ranked by how alone we'd be there)
Each carries the audience's own words, from comments under midlife-layoff videos. Quote none of them
verbatim in an ad; write the fictional equivalent. Comments about suicide exist in that data and are
excluded from this work entirely.
1. **Hoping to be laid off.** "Sometimes I hope for a layoff." "Praying for a package." Relief when
   it finally happens. The truth under it: outsourcing your courage to HR. (Stance 2.)
2. **The kitchen-table negotiation.** One partner's risk is the other partner's retirement. The
   embarrassed spouse; "she stayed when the ship crashed"; the spouse who stopped asking about work.
3. **The humiliation ladder, played with wit.** VP to deli counter. Trained by a 22-year-old who
   "speaks to me in the most condescending manner". Too experienced and not experienced enough at once.
4. **Corporate euphemism satire.** "You weren't laid off. You were transitioned to your next
   chapter." The one register this audience shares and laughs at.
5. **The meeting you weren't invited to,** told from the note-taker's side. Withholding built in.
6. **The logic trap.** "The people who want us to work until 70 won't hire us after 50."
7. **Golden handcuffs made literal.** The health-insurance bridge to 65 is the real reason people
   stay. COBRA, ACA, "coverage for my wife and me".
8. **Admitting you've gotten slower,** and why that's a reason to stop competing with 28-year-olds on
   their turf, not a reason to quit.
9. **Arguing with the skeptic on their terms.** "Most people shouldn't buy a franchise." "People
   don't sell profitable businesses." "The owner WAS the business." (Stance 1; the advisor as the one
   who says no. Anything about who pays the advisor needs FranChoice compliance review first.)
10. **The advisor with scar tissue.** Credibility, not story: our ads stay fiction, so this lives in
    the CTA voice, never as a character's biography.
**Grounding rule from the audience:** the corporate details must be right (PIP, FMLA, severance
weeks, COBRA windows, the badge that stops working at 5:01). This audience mocks stories that get
them wrong. Every such detail gets a source before generation.

## 4. Character rules
- **One person per ad**, 40–60, a man or a woman, in a corporate role we can see (the lanyard, the
  standing desk nobody raised, the parking spot with a name). Vary casting, age, body, region and
  culture across ads; make the cultural detail specific and true. Never a joke whose punchline
  depends on a racial stereotype. The joke lands on an *assumption* someone makes, not on a group.
- **Agency Hard Law.** The character is complicit in staying. There is an open door in the frame they
  could walk through. At least 60% of their lines have *them* as the subject: "I keep saying yes",
  never "they expect too much". Nothing is done *to* them that they didn't also choose.
- **The job is not the villain, and neither is the boss.** The 22-year-old manager is allowed to be
  right. The employer is allowed to be reasonable. The waiting is the antagonist.
- **Faces do less.** Dot eyes, a held blank stare, a body that tells it. No Pixar eyes, no tears to
  camera. Pain shows in objects and other people's lines ("It's like living with my sister").
- **Grown-up flirtation is allowed** and stays suggestive (Meta policy). Never near minors.
- **Kelsey's own arc is never a character.** If a story needs an advisor, the advisor is fiction and
  says no to someone at least once.

## 5. Arc shapes (compressions of the 12-beat chart; each ends on a decision, never an outcome)
Every arc obeys **pain → acknowledgment → decision → uncertain hope.** Hope is earned or it is cut.
The brand appears no earlier than ~75% and inside the world; the "product" never before the mentor.
The ending lands higher than the start, not back at it, and it is a *step*, not a result.
- **A. The Held Object (45–60s).** One object opens and closes the ad, changed: the badge, the
  lanyard, the parking sign, the login screen. Beats: object as it is (baseline) → three fast
  humiliations the character walked into (fall) → one line where they admit it out loud
  (acknowledgment) → the object handled differently (decision) → CTA inside the world.
- **B. The Refrain (30–45s).** One line said three times, meaning something different each time.
  "I'll think about it." First as a dodge to a spouse, then to the 22-year-old manager, last to
  themselves in a parked car, where it finally becomes true. The decision is the third reading.
- **C. The Other Side of the Door (30–60s).** Told by someone *else*: the note-taker in the meeting,
  the spouse at the table, the deli manager. The character is seen, not heard, until the last line,
  which is theirs. Withholding is the engine; the reveal is the decision.
Compression rule: which beats become one shot, which become one line, which are cut. Never keep
beats 1–5 (the wound) and skip straight to 11 (the elixir). That is the unearned bridge.

## 6. Devices (pick at least three; never all)
- **Bookend object** that changes (the Instagram crop, the ring, the second blanket).
- **A refrain that returns with new meaning.**
- **One accent colour with a job.** Grey can be the plot. The colour is a character.
- **One held shot of 8 seconds or more.** Stillness reads as confidence. AI cuts every 2–4s; we don't.
- **The medium admitted.** Thumbprints, paper grain, jittering lines, visible miniature scale.
- **Sound as a second idea.** A cheerful song over a sad picture; real rambling voices; silence.
- **Brand late and inside the world**: on a screen, a van, a napkin, not on a title card.
- **Withholding.** Show the box, not the layoff. Show the empty chair, not the meeting.
- **Every line is a picture.** "I'm the arm at the edge of the frame." "Sock lines by noon." "One tab
  still open." No line that could go on a greeting card. No pronoun fog. One idea per line.
- **Voice in the first 3 seconds; a screenshot-able line inside the first 20.**

## 7. Tone
Witty. Sarcastic. It pushes on uncomfortable truths: ageism after 45, the reorg email, loyalty that
isn't returned, the lie of passive income, the spouse who stopped asking. It never mocks the person
watching; it mocks the situation they both know. Dry beats loud. The funniest line is the truest one.
Dignity is non-negotiable: the character is never pathetic, only late.

## 8. The CTA (the only non-fiction in the ad)
One truthful line about what Waypoint does, in the same voice as the story, then one action. Every
factual claim about Waypoint or franchising is sourced before generation. The advisor says no to
most people; say so. Landing-page wording must match what the ad promised (open until Phase 3).
